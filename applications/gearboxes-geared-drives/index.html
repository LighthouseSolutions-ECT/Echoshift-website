Asset-specific diagnosis

A gearbox needs speed and ratio context

Gearboxes generate legitimate vibration at shaft and gear-mesh frequencies even when healthy. Useful diagnosis depends on knowing enough about the drive to distinguish expected transmission behaviour from meaningful change.

EchoShift can combine casing measurements with motor and driven-machine measurements to identify whether the gearbox is the likely source or simply transmitting vibration from elsewhere.

Especially useful when the drive sounds different

Changes in tonal noise, load-sensitive vibration, recurring bearing issues or a gearbox that behaves differently from a comparable unit are strong reasons for a structured snapshot.

What we investigate

What gearbox vibration can help investigate

Each pattern is interpreted with machine speed, measurement location and operating condition so the finding stays tied to the actual asset.

01

Gear-mesh change

Changes around mesh frequency and sidebands can indicate that the gear train deserves closer inspection.

02

Gearbox bearing condition

Higher-frequency and envelope evidence can be examined at accessible bearing locations.

03

Shaft-order behaviour

Input, intermediate and output shaft orders help relate vibration to rotating elements inside the drive.

04

Looseness / casing response

Mounting, foot or casing response can amplify or distort the apparent gearbox signature.

05

Load-sensitive vibration

Gear contact and structural response can change materially with load, so operating condition is recorded and compared where possible.

06

Vibration transmitted from elsewhere

Motor, coupling or driven-machine excitation can travel through the gearbox and must not be mistaken automatically for a gearbox fault.

Measurement strategy

Follow the transmission path

Measurements are selected around rigid input/output bearing regions and casing points, then compared with the motor and driven machine where useful. Known shaft speeds and ratios strengthen the analysis substantially.

Diagnostic boundary: A vibration snapshot can identify suspicious gearbox behaviour, but it cannot see tooth contact, lubrication condition or internal damage directly. Oil analysis, borescope inspection or strip-down may be the right confirmation step.

On-site process

What happens during a gearbox diagnostic

The measurement plan follows the mechanics of the asset and the maintenance question.

01

Map the drive

Capture motor speed, gearbox ratio, load, drive arrangement and any known tooth/bearing information.

02

Measure key casing positions

Take repeatable readings near the input/output bearing and transmission path.

03

Relate features to orders and mesh

Check shaft-related frequencies, mesh regions, sidebands, bearings and cross-machine agreement.

04

Escalate only when justified

Recommend inspection, oil/debris checks, alignment, bearing work, specialist gearbox assessment or monitoring according to the evidence.

Questions

Gearbox vibration FAQs

Scope the machine before attendance so the visit is useful.

Do you need the gearbox tooth count?

It helps. Shaft speeds, ratio and tooth counts make gear-mesh interpretation much stronger, but a useful first diagnostic can still be performed when some details are unavailable.

Can you identify a damaged gear tooth from one reading?

Sometimes a strong pattern can point toward gear damage, but a single snapshot should not be treated as direct visual proof. Load, sidebands, repeatability and other evidence matter.

Can gearboxes be monitored continuously?

Yes, suitable critical geared drives can be candidates for continuous monitoring when a one-off snapshot is not enough to manage the risk.
